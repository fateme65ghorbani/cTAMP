# cTAMP
## cTAMP: Sequence-Only Conditional Diffusion for Therapeutic Peptide Design Using Protein Language Model
Fatemeh Ghorbani1, Amir Lakizadeh
Designing peptide binders for protein targets remains a fundamental challenge, particularly for proteins lacking stable or experimentally resolved three-dimensional structures. Existing computational approaches largely depend on structural priors or operate in continuous embedding spaces without discrete token-level control, limiting their applicability to flexible or disordered targets. Here, we introduce cTAMP, a conditional Transformer–diffusion framework that enables structure‑independent, target‑aware peptide design using only protein sequence information. cTAMP encodes target proteins with a frozen pretrained ESM‑2 language model, providing rich contextual conditioning without any encoder fine‑tuning. A Transformer‑based peptide decoder is first pretrained under protein conditioning to enforce syntactic and biochemical validity, then kept frozen while peptide representations are refined through a conditional latent diffusion process under protein‑guided modulation via Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM). The model is optimized with a multi‑objective loss combining diffusion noise prediction, token‑level cross‑entropy, and contrastive semantic alignment. Extensive experiments demonstrate that cTAMP generates biologically realistic and target‑consistent peptides, achieving a positive contrastive alignment score (0.074) and high embedding‑space similarity to ground‑truth sequences with low variance. Critically, post‑hoc structural evaluation using AlphaFold‑Multimer reveals that 94.7% of generated complexes attain high local confidence (pLDDT > 70) and nearly half exhibit confident interfacial accuracy (ipTM > 0.45), confirming that sequence‑only conditioning suffices to produce structurally plausible peptide–protein interactions. Ablation studies further validate that the diffusion module is the key driver of conditional fidelity. cTAMP provides a scalable, structure‑free foundation for therapeutic peptide design against both well‑folded and intrinsically disordered targets.
2. Materials and Methods
2.1. Datasets
For this study, peptide–protein interaction data were assembled by integrating entries from the PepNN and Propedia repositories, both of which provide experimentally validated complexes [ ,]. To control for sequence redundancy among target proteins, clustering was performed using MMSeqs2 at an 80% sequence identity cutoff []. When multiple entries within the same cluster corresponded to identical binder peptide sequences, duplicate instances were removed to mitigate potential homology bias.Following this preprocessing pipeline, the resulting dataset contained 10,203 unique peptide–protein complexes. From these, 10,000 samples were randomly selected for model training, whereas 203 samples were set aside as a held-out test set. Additionally, 93 samples were partitioned from the training data to serve as a validation set. Both validation and test subsets were strictly excluded from the training process to ensure an unbiased assessment of model generalization.Each data instance was represented as a concatenated sequence comprising the target protein and its associated binder peptide. Sequence length constraints were applied to standardize model inputs, limiting binder peptides to a maximum of 50 amino acids and target proteins to a maximum of 1500 amino acids.This curation and partitioning strategy supports comprehensive evaluation of the model’s performance, assessing not only its capacity to recover established peptide–protein interactions but also its ability to generalize to unseen targets. The combination of redundancy filtering, controlled data splits, and explicit length thresholds establishes a reliable benchmark for sequence-conditioned peptide binder generation [34-36]. 
# 2.2. Methods
## Overview of the Proposed Framework (cTAMP)
To address the challenge of target-aware therapeutic peptide design, we introduce cTAMP, a Conditional Target-Aware Transformer–Diffusion framework for peptide generation. The primary objective of cTAMP is to generate biologically plausible, diverse, and target-conditioned peptide sequences explicitly tailored to a given receptor protein, in a structure-independent manner. Importantly, the target proteins can originate from viral, microbial, or human sources, allowing the framework to accommodate a broad spectrum of therapeutic applications.Unlike conventional antimicrobial peptide (AMP) prediction models or unconditional peptide generation approaches, cTAMP formulates peptide design as a fully conditional generative modeling task. In this framework, the target protein sequence is first encoded into contextual representations using a pretrained protein language model. These protein-derived embeddings serve as conditioning signals that guide a diffusion-based generative process operating in a continuous latent space. The refined latent peptide representations are subsequently decoded into discrete amino acid sequences using a Transformer-based decoder.
Through this sequential pipeline—comprising protein representation, conditional latent diffusion, and token-level decoding—cTAMP enables controlled and target-aware peptide generation while maintaining sequence realism and diversity.
The framework consists of three principal components:
⦁	A pretrained protein language model, which extracts rich residue-level embeddings from target protein sequences.
⦁	A conditional denoising diffusion module, performing stochastic refinement of peptide representations in latent space under explicit protein conditioning.
⦁	A decoder-only Transformer, which converts the denoised latent representations into biologically valid amino acid sequences.
By integrating pretrained protein representations with conditional diffusion modeling and token-level decoding, cTAMP captures both global protein–peptide compatibility and local sequence dependencies. Figure 1. provides an overview of the cTAMP model, highlighting its two main components: (a) the training pipeline and (b) the generation (inference) pipeline.(a) Training Phase:In this phase, the model learns to denoise peptide embeddings conditioned on a target protein. Inputs: A peptide sequence and a protein sequence are each passed through an ESM2 encoder to obtain their respective embeddings. Diffusion process on the peptide: The peptide embedding (x_0) is gradually corrupted by adding Gaussian noise, producing a noisy embedding x_t at timestep t. Conditioning: The protein embedding is used to compute FiLM scaling (gamma) and shifting (beta) parameters, which are applied to the noisy peptide embedding. Layer normalization and time embedding are also incorporated.· Denoising: A TransformerDenoiser (which includes a Transformer module and a pretrained decoder) attempts to reverse the diffusion step. Losses: Three losses guide training: Diffusion MSE loss: measures how well the model predicts the added noise.Token cross-entropy loss:evaluates the accuracy of reconstructed peptide tokens from the decoder’s logits. Contrastive loss: encourages similarity between the reconstructed peptide embedding and the original peptide embedding (both obtained from the ESM2 encoder).
(b) Generation PhaseHere, the trained model generates novel peptide sequences conditioned on a protein of interest, followed by a scoring step.· Input: Only a protein sequence is provided; it is encoded by the ESM2 encoder into a protein embedding. Reverse diffusion: Starting from pure Gaussian noise (x_T), the TransformerDenoiser iteratively applies reverse diffusion steps (t = T to 0). At each step, the noisy peptide representation x_t is modulated by the protein embedding via FiLM conditioning (gamma, beta) and combined with time embeddings.Decoder: The final denoised representation is fed into a pretrained decoder to produce logits over the vocabulary for each position, yielding a candidate peptide sequence. Contrastive similarity score: measures the alignment (similarity) between the embedding of the generated peptide (obtained by passing the generated peptide through ESM2) and the original peptide embedding (reference),Confidence PPL score (perplexity-based confidence),  AA balance score (amino acid composition bias check).Thus, part (a) focuses on training the denoising and generation capabilities, while part (b) depicts the generation/inference process where the trained model produces and filters candidate peptides.
